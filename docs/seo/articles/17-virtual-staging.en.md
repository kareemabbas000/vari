## Article

# Virtual Staging: What It Costs, What It Achieves and the Rules for 2026

Virtual staging means adding furniture and decor digitally to photos of an empty or dated room, so buyers can see how it could be lived in. It has become standard in property marketing because it is fast and costs a fraction of physical staging. It has also become regulated: in 2026, several markets require virtually staged photos to be labelled. This guide covers the numbers and the rules, from a studio that creates photorealistic interiors every day.

## Does staging work? What the data says

The best independent data comes from the National Association of Realtors (NAR) 2025 Profile of Home Staging, a survey of 1,266 agents carried out in February 2025.

- **83%** of buyers' agents said staging makes it easier for buyers to picture the property as their future home.
- **29%** of agents said staging increased the dollar value offered by 1% to 10%.
- **49%** of sellers' agents said staging reduced time on the market.
- The rooms buyers' agents rated most important to stage were the **living room (37%)**, the **primary bedroom (34%)** and the **kitchen (23%)**.

These are agents' opinions, not measured sales results. We found no independent study measuring the effect of virtual staging itself; widely repeated figures such as "40% more views" come from vendors. On virtual staging specifically, 24% of sellers' agents rated it as important as physical staging, and 34% as less important.

## Virtual staging vs real staging

**Virtual staging** is cheap, fast and flexible: you can show the same room as a family living room or a home office. Its limit is that buyers arrive at an empty home, so the photos must not promise more than the visit delivers.

**Physical staging** helps both the photos and the viewing, but costs more and takes time to install and remove.

**For homes that are not built yet**, neither applies. Off-plan sales need images created from the drawings, with the real layout and materials. That is architectural visualization, which we cover in our guide to [3D rendering for real estate developers](/en/blog/3d-rendering-for-real-estate-developers).

## The disclosure rules in 2026

**United States.** The NAR Code of Ethics (Article 12) requires agents to present "a true picture" in their advertising, and its Standard of Practice 12-10 forbids misleading images. On top of that:

- **California (AB 723)**: since **1 January 2026**, an ad that uses a digitally altered image must carry a conspicuous statement that it was altered and a link or QR code to the original, unaltered image. On a website the agent controls, the unaltered version must be included. Adding furniture, changing flooring, paint, landscaping or views counts as alteration; ordinary lighting, white-balance and cropping adjustments do not.
- **CRMLS** (California Regional MLS, rule 11.5.2): altered photos must be labelled, for example "Virtually Staged", and the original must be shown immediately before or after it. Real property elements such as floors, walls, landscaping or views must not be altered.
- **Stellar MLS** (Florida) allows virtual staging but forbids changing paint colours, replacing permanent fixtures, adding plants to the landscape, removing utility lines or covering up material defects.

**United Kingdom.** Since 6 April 2025, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 governs misleading property marketing, replacing the old Consumer Protection Regulations. An overall presentation that is likely to deceive can be unlawful even if each detail is accurate. Trading Standards guidance tells agents not to doctor photos in a way that misleads.

**European Union and Germany.** Misleading advertising is judged by the overall impression on the buyer (in Germany under §5 UWG). In addition, the transparency duties in Article 50 of the EU AI Act have applied since August 2026; press reports suggest AI-furnished property photos may need a visible label, although the details are still being clarified.

## Best practice for honest virtual staging

- **Label every virtually staged photo** on the image or right next to it.
- **Show the original** photo alongside it, even where the law does not yet require it.
- **Add furniture, not fixes.** Never change walls, floors, windows, views or defects.
- **Use furniture at a realistic size**, so rooms do not look larger than they are. We explain how images can mislead in [renders vs reality](/en/blog/renders-vs-reality).
- **Keep the style plausible** for the buyer the home will attract.

## FAQ

**How much does virtual staging cost?**
Human-designed virtual staging typically costs about US$16 to US$50 per photo, and AI tools under US$1 to about US$3 per photo, according to vendor price lists in 2026. Physical staging has a median cost of US$1,500 in the US (NAR, 2025).

**Is virtual staging legal?**
Yes, if it is not misleading. In California, since 1 January 2026, altered listing photos must be labelled and linked to the original. Many MLSs require a "virtually staged" label, and UK and EU consumer law forbids photos that create a misleading overall impression.

**Does virtual staging help sell a home?**
NAR's 2025 survey found that 83% of buyers' agents say staging makes it easier for buyers to picture a home as their own. We found no independent study that measures the effect of virtual staging on sale price.

**What is the difference between virtual staging and a 3D rendering?**
Virtual staging adds furniture to a photo of an existing room. A 3D rendering builds the whole space as a model, so it can show homes that are not built yet and any camera angle.

**Do I have to disclose virtual staging?**
In California and on most large MLSs, yes. Elsewhere it is the safest practice, because consumer protection law forbids misleading images.
