## Article

# Renders vs Reality: How to Read an Architectural Render Before You Buy

A render is not a photograph of a building that doesn't exist yet. It is an argument for one. Every image a visualization studio delivers is the result of dozens of decisions: where the camera stands, how wide it sees, what hour the sun sits at, which trees have grown, what has been left out. Most of those decisions are legitimate. Some quietly change what you think you are buying.

We make these images for a living, so this is a guide written from the other side of the screen. It is meant for buyers who are being shown a building through pictures, and for developers who want their pictures to hold up when the keys are handed over.

## Every render is an edited view

A photograph starts from reality and subtracts: it crops, it chooses a moment. A render starts from nothing and adds. Nothing appears in it unless someone put it there: a cloud, a car, a pot plant, the glow behind a window. That is why the useful question is never "is this render accurate?" but "which choices were made here, and do they change the facts I care about?"

The facts that matter are usually few: how big the rooms are, how much light reaches them, what the building looks like from the street on an ordinary day, what surrounds it, and what the finishes actually are. Every technique below touches one of them.

## The lens decides how big a room feels

Human vision concentrates on a fairly narrow field. Interior renders are often composed far wider, with focal lengths that would count as wide-angle on a real camera, because a wide lens shows more of the room in a single frame. It also stretches the foreground, pushes the back wall away and makes the space read larger than it will feel when you stand in it.

That is not automatically deceptive. A single image has to communicate a whole room, and photographers of real interiors use wide lenses for the same reason. The problem starts when the width is extreme, or when it is combined with furniture that is smaller than standard, a trick that makes floor area look generous.

Camera height matters as much. A camera placed low, at chest height or below, makes ceilings soar. A camera at standing eye level, with vertical lines kept vertical (what renderers call two-point perspective), tells you something close to what you will actually see.

Our own [Hearthstone Haven](/en/work/hearthstone-haven) living room is a good example of a deliberately wide, centred composition. The aim there was to show the full height of the vaulted timber roof and the stone chimney in one frame. If you were buying that room, you would want the plan beside the image to read the true depth.

**What to ask:** where was the camera, and at what height? A good studio can mark every camera position and field of view on the floor plan in minutes.

## The hour is chosen, and so is the glow

Look at almost any portfolio, ours included, and you will find buildings at dusk, or under a soft sky, with every window glowing amber. Our [Black Home](/en/work/black-home) exterior shows exactly that: an overcast afternoon, and warm light in every pane.

It is a convention for good reasons. Warm windows signal that a house is lived in, and they separate the façade from the sky. But by day, real glazing mostly reads dark or reflective from the outside. Architects interviewed about renderings have pointed out the same thing: in daylight, windows are typically black. So a glowing façade at midday is a mood, not a forecast.

The same goes for interior light. In [Penthouse in Dark Mood](/en/work/penthouse-in-dark-mood), the space is defined almost entirely by designed artificial light: recessed downlights, linear LED strips in the ceiling and along the stair, backlit display niches. That lighting is part of the interior design. Buyers should treat it as a specification and ask whether it is included, because without it the same room looks very different.

**What to ask:** which floor, which orientation, which hour? Is the lighting shown included in the price? Is there a daylight view?

## Trees take years to grow

Landscaping is where renders and reality drift furthest apart, simply because of time. A visualization usually shows planting the way the landscape architect intends it to look once established. On handover day, many of those trees will be young, staked and a fraction of the height.

In our [HNE Laboratory](/en/work/hne-laboratory) images for a project in Germany, the timber-clad building sits among mature conifers and autumn-coloured deciduous trees. Whether that is how the site looks on opening day depends on one fact a picture can't tell you: whether those trees already stand on the site or will be planted.

**What to ask:** which trees are existing and which are proposed? What size will the new ones be at planting?

## What isn't in the picture

Renders are tidy by default, because everything in them has to be modelled on purpose. That makes omission the most common way an image flatters, usually without anyone meaning to mislead:

Air-conditioning condensers on balconies and roofs. Sockets, switches, smoke detectors, sprinkler heads and access panels. Downpipes. The neighbouring building that will block part of the view, or the one that will be built next. Overhead cables, the car park, the bins.

None of these is a scandal on its own. Together they explain why so many buyers feel a finished building looks "busier" than the brochure. The professional standard is simple: the things that change a buyer's decision — a view, a neighbour, a dominant piece of equipment — should not disappear.

## Materials are perfect in a render

Digital stone has no lippage. Timber grain never repeats awkwardly. Grout lines are perfectly even, and every surface is clean. Rendering engines today simulate light on materials with remarkable physical accuracy, but the material itself is whatever the artist assigned. If the brochure shows a book-matched marble and the finishes schedule says "porcelain, marble-effect", both can be honest. The render just isn't the document that decides it.

**What to ask:** for the finishes schedule and, if possible, physical samples or a show apartment.

## When a render becomes a promise

Courts have started to treat renders as more than decoration. In Australia, *Ripani v Century Legend* concerned a luxury off-the-plan apartment whose marketing render showed a wide, flowing opening between the living area and the terrace. The trial judge found the render misleading under the Australian Consumer Law.

On appeal, the Full Federal Court ordered a new trial on one narrower question, whether the buyers continued to rely on the render. But it agreed that the "artist's impression" style disclaimers did not neutralise it ([2022] FCAFC 191). The lesson travels well beyond Australia: in an off-plan sale, the images stand in for an inspection, and a disclaimer in small print is not a licence to show something that will not be built.

Rules differ by country. In Dubai, for example, property advertising requires a permit from the regulator, and off-plan adverts carry their own disclosures. Check the current requirements with the authority or your agent rather than assuming a render is exempt.

## A short checklist before you sign

- Put the render next to the floor plan and the site plan, and find the camera.
- Ask which unit, floor and orientation the view represents.
- Ask for a daylight view, not only dusk.
- Ask which trees are existing and which are proposed.
- Ask what is not shown: neighbouring plots, rooftop equipment, services.
- Read the finishes schedule, not the image, for materials.
- Keep copies of the images you were shown when you bought.

## FAQ

**Are architectural renders accurate?**
They are accurate to the model and the decisions behind them. Geometry built from real drawings can be very precise. Lens choice, lighting, planting, context and omitted details are where an image can drift from what you will experience.

**What does "artist's impression" mean on a property advert?**
It signals that the image is illustrative. In at least one major court case it did not protect a developer whose render misrepresented the apartment, so buyers should still treat renders as part of what they were shown.

**Why do renders make rooms look bigger?**
Usually because of a wide focal length, a low camera, or furniture smaller than standard. Comparing the image with dimensioned floor plans shows the real proportions.

**Why are the windows lit in daytime renders?**
It is a stylistic convention that makes a building feel inhabited and separates the façade from the sky. In real daylight, most glazing looks dark or reflective from outside.

**Can I ask a developer for the camera positions of renders?**
Yes. Any studio working from a 3D model can show where each camera stands on the plan, and a developer confident in the images should have no difficulty sharing it.
